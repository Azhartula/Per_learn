# PerLearn closes AI schools’ personalization loop

## 1. Executive takeaway

AI video and AI-school platforms already do much of the operational work around personalized learning: they generate or select content, deliver it, test immediate understanding, update a mastery state, and choose what the learner sees next. **Their typical loop is “generate or select a lesson → watch or interact → quiz → remediate or advance,” but most systems personalize the topic, level, pace, language, or next activity—not the instructional method that produces durable learning.** The market is also split: products such as ibl.ai and AICoTeach combine video production with school delivery and assessment; HeyGen and Synthesia mainly generate videos; while Alpha/TimeBack, Synthesis, Primer, Khanmigo, and SchoolAI adapt learning without publicly claiming a unique generated video for every learner. PerLearn fits between the assessment boundary and the next content render: diagnose the misconception, select a bounded teaching treatment, send structured instructions to the host’s video generator, and judge the choice using a later unassisted transfer check. That is complementary to an NGEdu-like mastery and sequencing system, because **the host decides what the learner should learn next while PerLearn learns how the next explanation should be delivered**. The positioning must remain narrow and evidence-led: PerLearn is not a fixed “learning styles” classifier, and its current simulation is not proof of child learning gains. The strongest commercial entry is a low-risk pilot using pre-generated, approved variants for a few skills, with delayed transfer as the primary outcome and the buyer retaining curriculum, identity, rendering, safety controls, and parent reporting.

## 2. Their stack already closes the “what next?” loop

The cleanest way to understand today’s systems is from their own stack, not from PerLearn’s feature list. A topic, curriculum objective, teacher document, or learner need enters the system; the platform generates or selects a lesson; the student watches or interacts; an immediate quiz, exit ticket, or conversational check produces evidence; and a mastery or sequencing layer chooses remediation, review, or the next objective. Ulearn makes the sequence explicit—choose a topic, select a YouTube lesson, watch, answer video-grounded questions, then revisit material through spaced review—while TALA turns notes or documents into a narrated video plus quiz and flashcards, and Tutro turns a question, image, PDF, or link into a storyboarded lesson followed by an adaptive quiz ([Ulearn](https://www.ulearnai.org/); [TALA AI](https://talaai.io/ai-explainer-video-maker); [Tutro](https://tutro.ai/)). StudyAI similarly combines curriculum planning, lesson notes, stored quiz scores, review cards from missed questions, and optional narrated slides for difficult topics ([StudyAI](https://my-study-ai.com/for/homeschoolers)).

In full AI-school and homeschool systems, the front end varies but the decision loop is similar. Alpha/TimeBack records activity, accuracy, productive time, completion, XP, mastery, and test history, then routes learners through placement, sequencing, remediation, and review; importantly, it separates effort from mastery and can connect activity with MAP, state-test, SAT, and AP outcomes ([TimeBack: How It Works](https://docs.timeback.com/beta/about-timeback/how-it-works); [TimeBack XP System](https://docs.platform.timeback.com/getting-started/xp-system)). Synthesis embeds micro-assessments throughout its interactive math lessons, gives immediate guidance, fills gaps, and advances after demonstrated mastery ([Synthesis](https://www.synthesis.com/about)). Primer starts from a normed assessment, updates an academic model using exit tickets and app data, administers formal benchmarks three times a year, and combines algorithmic progress tracking with teacher intervention ([Primer Approach](https://primer.com/approach); [Primer founder interview](https://michaelbhorn.substack.com/p/inside-primers-growing-network)). NGEdu publicly claims curriculum-aligned interactive videos, AI tutoring, mastery gates, weak-concept detection, automatic difficulty changes, micro-lesson recommendations, and parent reporting, although its public material does not reveal whether videos are generated per learner, selected from a library, or pre-produced ([NextGen Schooling](https://www.ngedu.com.au/); [NGEdu launch announcement](https://www.skillihire.com/blog/ng-edu-ai-homeschooling-australia-launch)).

This means prospective partners do not need another generic sequencer. **They already try to answer “what skill, resource, or difficulty comes next?”** Their exposed dashboards are strongest at showing what a learner did, how accurately, for how long, what they have mastered, and what comes next. Even sophisticated implementations rarely show which explanation caused understanding, whether it endured, or whether another instructional treatment would work better. TimeBack itself delegates subject-specific pedagogy and learning experience design to integrated apps, making the distinction especially clear: the platform can own placement and mastery infrastructure without owning the best way to explain a particular concept to a particular learner ([TimeBack integration levels](https://docs.timeback.com/beta/build-on-timeback/integration-levels)).

## 3. Named platforms occupy different layers of the stack

**NGEdu / NextGen Schooling** is the most direct pitch target because it presents an integrated Australian homeschool experience: prepared lessons, interactive videos, AI conversation, games, quizzes, mastery gates, weak-area detection, and family reporting. Its marketing implies a strong “what next?” layer, but there is no public API, event schema, learner-model documentation, independent outcome evidence, or confirmation that its videos are dynamically generated ([NextGen features](https://www.ngedu.com.au/features); [NGEdu launch announcement](https://www.skillihire.com/blog/ng-edu-ai-homeschooling-australia-launch)). PerLearn should therefore treat NGEdu’s actual architecture as a discovery question, not pitch an assumed integration as fact.

**ibl.ai Agentic Video** is the clearest K–12 video-generation match. It turns a script, lesson plan, or outline into age-appropriate AI-instructor video, can draft scenes and storyboards, supports generated visual sequences, publishes to Google Classroom or district LMSs through LTI 1.3, and advertises assessments, grade passback, and student analytics ([ibl.ai Agentic Video](https://ibl.ai/product/agentic-video/k12)). **AICoTeach** similarly combines avatar-teacher lessons, multilingual delivery, subtitles, branching assessments, auto-grading, parent progress tools, and low-bandwidth delivery ([AICoTeach](https://aicoteach.com/)). These are natural integration targets because they already control generation and assessment; the missing public capability is a longitudinal policy that changes the teaching treatment because earlier treatments produced better delayed mastery.

**HeyGen, Synthesia, Fliki, and KreadoAI** are primarily production systems. They vary avatars, voices, scripts, language, pacing, layouts, captions, and branding; Synthesia can export SCORM and HeyGen can localize a lecture without reshooting ([HeyGen course creation](https://www.heygen.com/course-creation); [Synthesia SCORM](https://www.synthesia.io/post/scorm); [Fliki education](https://fliki.ai/use-cases/education-and-e-learning); [KreadoAI education](https://www.kreadoai.com/education)). These controls are generally selected by an author, not autonomously chosen from evidence that a method improved an individual learner’s retention. For these vendors, PerLearn would need either an LMS/school partner supplying learner and assessment data or an expanded product layer around their rendering API.

**Alpha/TimeBack, Primer, and Synthesis** are adaptive learning or school-operating systems rather than per-student video generators. TimeBack has the richest disclosed event and mastery infrastructure; Primer combines placement, goals, dashboards, benchmarks, and human teachers; Synthesis adapts guidance and interactive representations within K–5 math. Their personalization is substantive, but public documentation does not show a learner-specific policy that records “a visual explanation failed, a worked example succeeded, and later transfer confirmed the difference” ([TimeBack: How It Works](https://docs.timeback.com/beta/about-timeback/how-it-works); [Primer Approach](https://primer.com/approach); [Synthesis](https://www.synthesis.com/about)).

**Khanmigo and SchoolAI** personalize conversational support. Khanmigo provides Socratic help around Khan Academy exercises, articles, and existing videos, while SchoolAI Spaces can vary examples, reading level, vocabulary, feedback, language, and difficulty and surface misconceptions to teachers ([Khan Academy Districts](https://blog.khanacademy.org/becoming-a-khan-academy-districts-partner/); [SchoolAI personalization](https://schoolai.com/blog/personalize-at-scale-with-schoolai)). **Omega, StarSpark, AdaptEd, and Lemonæ** are strategically closer to PerLearn’s concept. Omega documents worked examples, a hint ladder, blind mastery questions, misconception mapping, and a review roughly two weeks later; StarSpark claims misconception-triggered reteaching and older-concept review; AdaptEd claims misconception-specific visual/voice reteaching and retesting; and Lemonæ claims knowledge-graph-driven variation of visuals, voice, animation, examples, and contexts ([Omega School](https://omega.foundation/programs/tutoring); [StarSpark](https://starspark.ai/homeschool); [AdaptEd](https://learnadapted.com/); [Lemonæ](https://lemonae.ai/)). These products validate buyer interest in the problem, but the smaller vendors’ method-adaptation claims lack independent outcome validation, and none publicly documents the complete per-learner experimental loop PerLearn proposes.

## 4. Personalization stops before causal method learning

“Personalization” currently covers at least four different capabilities: media localization such as avatar and language; teacher-authored differentiation such as reading level; session adaptation such as the next hint or question; and longitudinal pathing such as placement, mastery, and curriculum sequence. **The dominant stopping point is selecting the right skill, activity, support level, and pace—not estimating which pedagogy causes durable mastery for this learner in this concept and mastery state.** Synthesis clearly offers multiple modalities and tailored guidance, but its public pages do not establish that a prior failure causes a measured switch among story, diagram, manipulative, or worked-example treatments. TimeBack prescribes strong instructional principles such as retrieval practice, worked examples, rapid error correction, mastery gates, and spaced review, but these are platform-wide design constraints rather than a per-student method-selection policy ([TimeBack principles](https://docs.timeback.com/beta/about-timeback/principles); [TimeBack non-negotiables](https://docs.timeback.com/beta/about-timeback/concepts/non-negotiables)).

Assessment is the second boundary. Most systems rely on immediate correctness, task completion, exit tickets, conversation evidence, or current mastery gates. Those signals are useful for diagnosing what is weak, but **an immediate correct answer does not establish retention or isolate which explanation caused it**. Alpha’s external MAP testing and Primer’s periodic benchmarks measure longer-term growth, while Omega is unusually explicit about a later review date; none of these publicly attributes later performance to a particular instructional representation at the individual level ([Alpha/TimeBack parent guide](https://support.alpha.school/api/v1/articles/134425/attachments/1949856/download); [Primer Approach](https://primer.com/approach); [Omega School](https://omega.foundation/programs/tutoring)).

The key whitespace is therefore not “more multimodal content.” A catalog containing visual, spoken, story-based, and worked-example versions remains static unless learner evidence changes the selection. Nor should PerLearn infer a permanent “visual learner” or “auditory learner.” Reviews find insufficient evidence for fixed learning-style matching, and a 2024 meta-analysis found only limited crossover evidence while warning that study quality did not support broad adoption ([Learning-styles mini-review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6113575/); [2024 meta-analysis](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2024.1428732/full)). The defensible claim is conditional and revisable: **method X has produced the best later, unassisted performance so far for this learner, concept, and mastery state**.

## 5. PerLearn plugs in at the mastery gate

PerLearn belongs after an initial exposure and around the host’s mastery gate. The host sends a pseudonymous learner identifier, target skill, current mastery estimate, prior attempts, misconception tags, age or grade constraints, accessibility needs, and the approved treatment variants. PerLearn uses a short diagnostic to estimate the likely misconception, then returns a method decision—such as worked example first, problem first, visual explanation, or real-world story/analogy—plus structured generation controls. Those controls should specify instructional sequence, example count and type, visual schema, narration density, misconception contrast, prerequisite reminder, practice transition, duration, and safety constraints. The host’s generator then renders or retrieves the lesson, preserving ownership of curriculum, content delivery, identity, and parent records.

During delivery, the host can record completion, seeking, replay, pauses, speed, tutor questions, and abandonment. These are useful context and engagement signals, **but they should not be treated as proof of learning**. An immediate diagnostic then updates the learner’s concept and misconception state. Later, after spacing and without hints, an isomorphic or transfer item supplies the reward used to update the teaching-method policy. That separation matters: immediate responses answer “what does the learner appear to understand now?”, while delayed unassisted responses answer “did this treatment produce learning that survived beyond the lesson?”

Technically, this resembles an established adaptive-service integration. Knewton’s partner model had hosts send content metadata and learner interaction events, after which the adaptive layer estimated knowledge state and returned recommendations and analytics ([Knewton API overview](https://dev.knewton.com/implementation/api-overview/); [Knewton events](https://dev.knewton.com/implementation/sending-events/)). Educational contextual-bandit research has also represented prior concept knowledge as context, a video, question, or simulation as the action, and future assessment performance as reward ([EDM 2016](https://www.educationaldatamining.org/EDM2016/proceedings/paper_18.pdf); [ICASSP 2017](https://doi.org/10.1109/icassp.2017.7953377)). PerLearn’s API boundary can therefore remain compact: diagnosis, treatment recommendation, immutable event ingestion, and delayed reward attribution. Stable versioning across learner, skill, content, method, policy, generator model, assessment item, and recommendation is essential; without it, neither PerLearn nor the buyer can distinguish method effects from easier tests, content defects, or generator changes.

The ownership line should be explicit. **The platform owns “what”: standards, prerequisites, curriculum sequence, schedules, rendering, delivery, identity, and parent-facing records. PerLearn owns “how”: misconception-aware diagnostics, constrained treatment selection, delayed reward attribution, and the generation instructions derived from that evidence.** Safety policy, content QA, experiment governance, and outcome reporting are shared. PerLearn should augment rather than automatically replace the host’s conventional quiz, because platforms still need comparable scores for mastery records, reporting, and compliance.

## 6. The wedge is measurable lift, not another personalization claim

The best initial wedge is a bounded experiment, not a demand to replace the buyer’s adaptive stack. Choose one subject, one age band, three to five skills, and several human-approved or pre-generated treatment variants. Begin in shadow mode to validate event joins and establish baseline rates; then compare the buyer’s existing rule with a safety-constrained contextual policy while holding objectives, difficulty, duration, vocabulary, factual content, and assessment blueprint as constant as possible. Use delayed unassisted transfer as the primary outcome, with completion, abandonment, hint use, subgroup performance, factual errors, and generation cost as guardrails. Dynamic per-learner rendering should follow only if the treatment lift survives holdout evaluation.

The first objection will be, “We already personalize.” The answer is concrete: their system chooses skill, level, pace, or difficulty; PerLearn estimates the effect of the explanation treatment while leaving those controls intact. The second objection—“Where is the real-world evidence?”—is valid. PerLearn’s present simulation demonstrates software behavior, not real child learning gains. Bandits do not guarantee material improvement: an ASSISTments support-selection implementation found an initial Thompson-sampling method only slightly better than random, while a contextual method produced stronger simulated results ([Automatic Interpretable Personalized Learning](https://doi.org/10.1145/3491140.3528267)). The response should be a preregistered pilot, confidence intervals, randomized holdouts, and willingness to stop if lift is absent.

Operational objections are equally important. Exploration can expose a child to a weaker lesson, so only approved variants should be explored, regret should be capped, repeated failure should trigger fallback, and high-stakes cases should allow teacher or parent override. Generated variants can differ in more than teaching method, so every artifact and model version needs logging and QA. Extra testing can hurt engagement, so diagnostics should use one or two discriminating items and delayed checks should be embedded in normal review. Rendering can be slow or expensive, so the first deployment should cache variants and generate asynchronously. Child-data procurement requires pseudonymous IDs, minimization, regional and retention controls, deletion/export, encryption, auditability, subprocessor transparency, and no training on customer data; UNESCO specifically highlights privacy, age appropriateness, validation, and human oversight for generative AI in education ([UNESCO guidance](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research)).

The strategic threat is that a video platform can build this itself. Tutro already controls generated lessons, adaptive quizzes, telemetry, and alternate explanations, while NGEdu claims weak-area detection and micro-lesson recommendations ([Tutro](https://tutro.ai/); [NGEdu launch announcement](https://www.skillihire.com/blog/ng-edu-ai-homeschooling-australia-launch)). PerLearn’s defensibility therefore cannot be “we personalize.” It must become **faster, safer experimentation; diagnosis quality; clean delayed-outcome attribution; interpretable treatment evidence; cross-generator integration; and a growing, versioned map of which instructional treatments work under which learner and concept conditions**.

## 7. Recommended pitch line for NGEdu-like and AI-video school buyers

### For NGEdu-like homeschool and mastery platforms

> **“You already know what each student should learn next. PerLearn adds the missing ‘how’: it diagnoses the misconception at your mastery gate, selects an approved explanation treatment for the next lesson, and verifies days later—without hints—which treatment actually produced durable understanding.”**

This line respects the buyer’s existing curriculum, sequencing, difficulty, and reporting stack. It also avoids claiming that NGEdu definitely generates video dynamically, a fact its public materials do not establish. In discovery, the founder should ask where videos come from, which event and assessment data are available, how mastery is defined, whether delayed review already exists, and whether the platform can render or retrieve multiple approved versions of one objective.

### For AI-video generation and school-delivery platforms

> **“Your system can generate, deliver, and quiz an AI lesson. PerLearn closes the learning loop: it turns assessment evidence into the next video’s teaching strategy, then attributes later unassisted mastery back to that strategy so every new render is informed by what actually worked.”**

**Cost wedge (say this explicitly):** Long generic videos burn render budget and attention. Evaluation-driven personalization means the next video is short and specialised for *this* student’s gap and the explanation style that works for them — so platforms generate fewer wasted minutes per mastered skill, not more content for its own sake.

The shortest category statement is: **“PerLearn is the treatment-selection and delayed-evaluation layer for AI-generated lessons — so the next video is personal, shorter, and cheaper to justify.”** The strongest commercial promise is not a universal learning-style profile. It is a controlled, auditable way for a platform to learn whether worked examples, problem-first instruction, visuals, stories, or future treatment variants improve durable mastery for a particular learner in a particular context.

## Conclusion

The market has assembled nearly every component of an adaptive AI school—content generation, tutoring, mastery maps, dashboards, sequencing, assessments, and increasingly rich telemetry—but the components are optimized mainly around production and proximal performance. PerLearn’s opportunity is to connect them through a method-level learning loop: diagnose, choose a bounded treatment, generate, measure transfer after delay, and update the policy.

That opportunity is real but narrow. The winning product will look less like a new school and more like measurement infrastructure embedded inside existing schools: conservative with child experimentation, explicit about uncertainty, compatible with the buyer’s existing mastery model, and credible enough to show incremental lift under controlled conditions. If PerLearn proves that lift, it gives AI-video platforms something their rendering engines alone cannot supply—evidence about how to teach, not merely the capacity to generate.
